# Explainability and error analysis (Phase 8)

_Generated by `scripts/phase8_report.py` from the results files named in each section. Validation is the primary split (rule 8); test repeats are written as "val / test". Figures in `figures/` (`*_val.png`, `*_test.png`)._

> **Explanations show what a model uses, not what causes collaborations.** A feature that a model relies on can be a proxy (for activity, team membership, or a sampling artefact of the evaluation), and correlated features share credit arbitrarily.

> **Scope.** "Headline model" means the pair-focused LightGBM, the model pre-specified as the headline before the final-pass check. SHAP, the error analysis and the calibration below were run on it. A logistic regression trained on the same pairs scores about the same (test macro hard_active 0.808 against 0.799; exploratory, `results/pf_logreg_exploratory.csv`), so the gain comes from the training pairs, not the learner. Its explanations were not computed; because the LightGBM is additive (2-leaf trees), its drivers are likely similar, but that is not checked.

## 1. What the models rely on (SHAP; `results/shap_importance.csv`, `figures/shap_*`)

- **Headline model (pair-focused LightGBM)**, top features by mean |SHAP| on validation hard_active pairs: `rw_aa` (0.48), `nbr_text_sim_max` (0.37), `career_age_min` (0.15), `resource_allocation` (0.14), `text_cos` (0.13), `years_since_last_max` (0.12). It uses 2-leaf trees, so it is **additive**: every SHAP interaction value is exactly 0 (max 0.0e+00). Each feature's effect is a step function, visible as vertical bands in `figures/shap_beeswarm_val.png`.
- **General LightGBM**: `katz` (1.24), `nbr_text_sim_max` (1.11), `text_cos` (0.67), `recent_works_max` (0.57), `distance` (0.48), `nbr_text_sim_min` (0.44). Its strongest interactions are `katz` × `text_cos` and `katz` × `nbr_text_sim_max` (`figures/shap_interactions_val.png`).
- **SHAP and gain importance mostly agree** (Spearman of the feature rankings, validation hard_active: headline 0.89, general 0.98). The disagreement is in the share given to the largest feature: gain counts how much each split reduces training loss, so `rw_aa`, which the additive model uses in most of its trees, gets more credit by gain than by SHAP (table below).

**Regrouping by meaning changes the story.** Filed by code block, the headline model looks like a "temporal" model (0.474 / 0.471 of |SHAP|). ## 2. The activity detector (`results/activity_detector.csv`, `figures/activity_detector_*.png`)

Pooled `hard` negatives are not matched on activity, so a model can win there by recognising authors who keep publishing. Three pieces of evidence:

1. **A single activity feature is a strong scorer on pooled hard and useless once activity is matched.** `recent_works_min` scores 0.716 / 0.749 ROC-AUC on pooled hard but 0.463 / 0.488 on macro hard_active; `−years_since_last_max` 0.728 / 0.753 → 0.485 / 0.495 (val / test).
2. **The general model's activity attributions flip from informative to misleading.** Its author-activity SHAP discriminates on pooled hard (0.721 / 0.742) and points the wrong way on hard_active (0.391 / 0.406). The headline model is the mirror image (0.338 / 0.309 on pooled hard, 0.641 / 0.627 on hard_active): trained among active authors, it uses activity and career only to rank within them.
3. **Partial dependence vs observed rate** (`figures/activity_detector_val.png`, `report/shap/partial_dependence_val.csv`, validation): on pooled hard pairs the observed positive rate rises with the less active author's recent works (0.26 → 0.64 from the lowest to the highest bin) and collapses with years since the last work (0.52 → 0.05). On hard_active pairs the rate instead FALLS with recent works (0.60 → 0.38). The general model's partial dependence rises with recent works on both sets (0.81 → 0.90 and 0.90 → 0.95): right on pooled hard, wrong among active pairs.

So activity features "dominate" pooled hard because it is partly an activity-prediction task; within activity-matched groups there is little left for them to explain.

## 3. The distance shortcut (`results/gnn_explanations.csv`, `results/gnn_shortcut_check.csv`, `figures/gnn_explanations_val.png`)

Training on distance-2 negatives against positives at any distance lets distance alone separate the classes. The shortcut check (test ROC-AUC of far random negatives against distance-2 positives; > 0.5 = shortcut) is 1.000 for the pre-fix pair-focused LightGBM, 0.015 after the fix, and 0.775 for the best GNN, which was never retrained.

**CPU retrain for explanations.** No weights were saved on Colab, so both GNNs were retrained on CPU from their seed-0 configs (the pair-focused one on its original, shortcut training set). Fidelity: Spearman with the Colab validation scores 0.987 (pair-focused) and 0.955 (general); validation shortcut check 0.780 retrained vs 0.791 Colab.

**What the explanations show (8 validation predictions per model):**

- **The pair-focused GNN treats a shared co-author as evidence against a link.** Deleting every u–w–v path through a common neighbour *raises* its logit for its confident true and false positives (+0.71, +0.74, +0.77, +0.54). For the general GNN the same occlusion changes the logit by -0.50 to -0.00: shared paths count weakly in favour.
- **Its most confident predictions are far pairs.** The two highest-scored validation random negatives at distance ≥ 3 (both disconnected, no common neighbour) get logits 8.2 and 7.8, above every hard_active pair (its best true positive: 0.89). Integrated gradients put that score on the endpoints' own features (+2.75) and their neighbourhoods (+4.97), with no connecting structure.
- **GNNExplainer was not informative here.** Its node-feature masks for the pair-focused model are close to uniform (about 0.25 per feature) and its edge masks spread over 2-hop subgraphs of up to ~150k edges, so the figure's top-15 edges mostly show one endpoint's star. Integrated gradients (completeness error ≤ 0.12 logits) and edge occlusion were used for the conclusions above.

**Failure patterns and hypotheses** (exploratory checks, raw p, not in the Holm family; val / test):

- **H1. False positives look like collaborators: dense, recent shared neighbourhoods and similar topics.** Supported for established pairs (one-sided Mann-Whitney, FP vs true positives, more common neighbours: p = 6e-12 / 5e-13), **not** for newcomer pairs (p = 1.00 / 0.98). Many established false positives are plausible collaborations that simply had not happened yet (right-censoring, below).
- **H2. Missed collaborations are topically distant.** Supported in both groups: missed positives have lower text similarity than other positives (p ≤ 1e-21) and are more often cross-field (Fisher p = 2e-06 / 0.0003 established, 0.013 / 0.00042 newcomer). The model misses collaborations that the network and topic signals give no hint of.
- **H3. Performance by common-neighbour tercile is not monotone.** Established ROC-AUC low / mid / high 0.80 / 0.88 / 0.78 (val), 0.78 / 0.83 / 0.76 (test). Hardest is the top tercile, where non-collaborators also share many co-authors (large groups), and the bottom tercile, where there is little structure to go on.

**Cross-discipline pairs are not a weak spot.** ROC-AUC on same-field pairs 0.814 / 0.792 vs cross-field 0.848 / 0.830 (val / test; general LightGBM 0.724 vs 0.767, Adamic-Adar 0.700 vs 0.747 on val). Cross-field pairs are 49% of new collaborations and 60% of activity-matched non-collaborations (val), so being cross-field is itself weakly informative. **Definition caveat:** one modal OpenAlex field per author from 26 fields is coarse (half of all new collaborations cross a "field"); a finer definition (subfield or topic overlap) could change this.

**Right-censoring (limitation).** Of the established top-100 validation false positives (prediction year 2024), 4 co-author in 2025, against 0.7% of all established negatives; newcomer false positives: 0. Some "errors" are early, not wrong, but only one later year exists, so this is a small, lower-bound effect and not a reason to rescore. For test (prediction year 2025) the data contain no later year, so it cannot be checked.

**Newcomers (parked since Phase 5).** With group-matched negatives, no model does genuinely better on newcomer pairs: on test every model's newcomer score is at or below its established score, and the only positive gap on validation (logistic regression, hard_active) does not repeat on test. ## 5. Calibration (`results/calibration.csv`, `results/calibrated_two_stage*.csv`, `figures/calibration_*.png`)

Seed-averaged probabilities on the evaluation pairs (val / test):

| model | pairs | Brier | Brier of constant base rate | ECE (10 bins) | mean prediction vs positive rate (val) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| headline | hard_active | 0.218 / 0.230 | 0.250 | 0.204 / 0.214 | 0.30 vs 0.50 |
| headline | hard | 0.185 / 0.196 | 0.238 | 0.109 / 0.127 | 0.28 vs 0.39 |
| general | hard_active | 0.421 / 0.417 | 0.250 | 0.432 / 0.428 | 0.93 vs 0.50 |
| general | hard | 0.381 / 0.354 | 0.238 | 0.436 / 0.410 | 0.83 vs 0.39 |

**The scores cannot be read as probabilities.** The general LightGBM predicts about 0.9 for distance-2 pairs of which half collaborate (it was trained against random pairs, where any shared co-author is strong evidence), and its Brier score is worse than always predicting the base rate. The headline model under-predicts (trained at 1:3). Even a perfectly calibrated model here would give probabilities for a ~1:1 sampled evaluation set; real new collaborations are a tiny fraction of all pairs. Use the scores for ranking only.

**Calibrating the two-stage model does not close its gap.** With both components Platt-scaled on training-split out-of-fold predictions, the two-stage model scores 0.845 / 0.851 on pooled hard (uncalibrated 0.848 / 0.854; general LightGBM 0.861 / 0.864); test difference vs the general model -0.013, Holm p = 0.0068 (34-test family). The product's weakness is not miscalibration; separately trained components lose the interaction between activity and the pair signal.

## 6. RQ3 answer

*Which features drive predictions, and where do models fail (e.g. newcomers, cross-discipline pairs)?*

- **Drivers.** Among friend-of-friend pairs who are both active, the headline model relies on recency-weighted shared neighbourhoods (time-weighted structure, 0.288 / 0.281 of |SHAP|) and topical similarity of the authors and their co-author circles (0.279 / 0.281), then plain structure (0.247 / 0.248) and career stage / activity (0.186 / 0.190). The general model leans on Katz, text and recent output, and part of what looks like skill on pooled protocols is detecting active authors and far-vs-near pairs.
- **Failures.** (1) Newcomer pairs are harder for every model. (2) Collaborations with no network or topical precursor are missed (H2). (3) Established pairs in dense, topically close neighbourhoods produce confident false positives, a few of which collaborate a year later (H1, censoring). (4) Cross-field pairs, by a coarse field definition, are not a weak spot. (5) Models trained or evaluated with mismatched negatives learn shortcuts: activity on pooled hard, distance in the pair-focused GNN.

## 7. If this were used as a collaboration recommender

- It would recommend people who already share recent co-authors and work on similar topics: it reinforces existing clusters and will rarely surface the cross-topic, no-shared-contact collaborations it misses.
- It is a within-friend-of-friend ranker. It should rank candidates two steps away, not score arbitrary pairs (random-pair ROC-AUC is lower than the general model's), and its scores are not probabilities.
- Newcomers are served worse; recommendations to early-career researchers deserve the least trust.
- Author-name disambiguation errors propagate into the graph; the sensitivity check (Phase 7) kept the model ranking but removed almost half of the positives.
- Explanations of the form "you share N recent co-authors and similar topics" are faithful to what the model uses, but they describe correlation, not a reason two people should collaborate.
